Of all the stages in restoring water damage, drying the structure counts most, and it is the stage most often botched by people without training. Extraction takes care of standing water, but drywall, framing, subflooring, insulation, concrete and other building materials stay loaded with trapped moisture. This trapped moisture must be scientifically removed through controlled evaporation and dehumidification to prevent the mold growth, wood rot, material degradation and odor problems that develop when structures remain wet.

Muskegon Restoration employs IICRC-certified structural drying technicians who understand psychrometry, the study of temperature, humidity and moisture relationships in air. That knowledge lets them work out where equipment goes, what temperature to hold and how air should move so evaporation is as effective as possible on every job. Because hardwood, drywall, plaster and concrete do not dry at the same speed, the method has to change with the material to bring it to acceptable moisture content without harming it further.

Daily monitoring is built into how we dry a structure, and it relies on calibrated meters, thermal cameras and thermo-hygrometers. We document moisture readings throughout the drying process, creating a detailed drying log that shows progressive moisture reduction in all affected materials. Those readings guide daily equipment adjustments that keep the drying as efficient as it can be. A surface that feels dry to your hand proves nothing, so the job is finished only once all affected materials test within the acceptable standards set by IICRC S500.

When to Call for Structural Drying in Muskegon

Recognizing the warning signs early can save you time, money, and prevent significant damage to your property. Watch for these telltale signs that professional help is needed:

  • Window glass that fogs up near the affected rooms
  • Wall finishes that start to peel or bubble in the days after water damage
  • Signs of deterioration where an old leak was left to dry on its own
  • Drywall that is soft, swollen or shows moisture staining
  • High humidity in affected areas despite efforts to ventilate
  • Trapped moisture often announces itself with a persistent musty odor after cleanup
  • Ceilings, floors or walls that still read high on a moisture meter following a leak

How to Prevent Structural Drying Problems

A little prevention goes a long way in avoiding expensive restoration emergencies. Here are our expert recommendations:

  • Avoid moving the machines yourself, because their placement follows our moisture map
  • New odors or wet areas during the drying period should be reported to your technician right away
  • Let air movers and dehumidifiers run continuously, 24/7, until the technician removes them
  • Deal with water intrusion right away, since an early start on drying gives a better result
  • Do not place personal items on top of drying equipment or block the airflow from air movers
  • Moisture can sit deep in a material whose surface feels dry, so rely on the meter readings

Why Professional Structural Drying Matters

There is real science behind structural drying, which is why guessing does not work. Secondary mold damage most often traces back to someone who put down fans and hoped things would dry. To do it professionally, you have to know how each material takes on and gives up moisture, work out the grain depression, meaning the moisture removal capacity, for the job, and check progress every day. Lacking that understanding, people either stop too soon and leave moisture for mold, or dry too hard and harm wood and other materials.

Professional drying equipment is not a bigger version of what stores sell, it is a different kind of machine. High-velocity air movers produce precise airflow patterns that speed up evaporation from the structure, and low-grain refrigerant (LGR) commercial dehumidifiers capture 10 to 20 times the moisture a home unit can. Between the investment in equipment and the training needed to run it, it is no surprise that DIY drying falls well short of a professional job.
